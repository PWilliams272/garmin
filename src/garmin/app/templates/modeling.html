Overview

This page documents how strength progression and wellness response are modelled in this repo — what the models do, what evidence motivated each choice, and where they currently fall short. It is a working notebook, not a results announcement: one of the two models does not yet converge, and that is stated plainly rather than hidden.

On originality. Almost everything here is standard method applied to one person's data. The load–rep power law is Lombardi's; the envelope fit is Yu & Moyeed's Bayesian quantile regression; the latent level is a textbook local-level state space. What was actually assembled here is small — mainly a data-quality trick for classifying what Garmin's weight column means, and the particular way these standard parts are combined. Section 10 itemises every piece and its source.

Primer · the three moving parts

The model is built from three ideas. None is complicated on its own, and each exists to solve a specific problem with the data. Play with the sliders — the shapes are the explanation.

1. A lopsided error bar, so the fit rides the top of the cloud

Ordinary regression assumes being above the line is as surprising as being below it. That is wrong here: you routinely lift less than you could (warmups, back-off sets), but you can't lift more than you can. Missing low is normal; missing high is nearly impossible.

So the error distribution is deliberately lopsided. Drag q upward and watch the left tail (below the line) go long and cheap while the right tail (above) gets steep and expensive. The fit then settles near the top of the data rather than through its middle.

Literature The Asymmetric Laplace, standard for Bayesian quantile regression (Yu & Moyeed 2001).

2. A squash, so a parameter can't wander somewhere impossible

The sampler explores freely and will happily propose a rep-decay exponent of 0.6, which would mean a 10-rep max is a quarter of your 1RM. Bodies don't do that. Rather than reject such proposals — which makes sampling lurch — the unconstrained number is passed through an S-shaped squash that maps anything onto the plausible range.

Note the shape: near the middle it is nearly a straight line, so the data moves β freely where it matters. Only at the extremes does it flatten and refuse to go further.

3. A wandering line, so strength can change without being told how

Strength isn't a straight line, and it isn't a fixed shape. Rather than assume a trend, the model lets a line wander: each step is a small random nudge from the last. Given data it follows the data; given a gap it simply keeps its last value and widens its uncertainty.

The slider sets how big each nudge may be. Too small and the line can't keep up with real change; too large and it chases noise. That size is itself estimated from the data.

Literature A local-level state space / Gaussian random walk — textbook time series (Harvey 1989).

1 · The data

Everything is pulled from Garmin Connect into a curated parquet layer. Two families matter here: strength sets (one row per set: exercise, reps, weight) and daily wellness (HRV, sleep, resting HR, stress, body battery).

The binding constraint on anything wellness-related is that those signals only begin in December 2022. Activities go back to 2016, but HRV does not, so every training→recovery model is limited to roughly the last three and a half years.

How sessions are structured

Sets aren't always done in blocks. A superset alternates between two exercises — bench, curl, bench, curl — which means the set next to a given set is a different movement. That breaks any rule that reads neighbouring sets as context, and it is not a rare edge case here.

Rep tempo, and the sets where the watch was left running

Individual rep timings aren't recorded, but each set's total duration is — and divided by reps that gives a tempo, which is a real signal of how hard a set was. It needs cleaning first: sometimes the watch keeps running into the rest period, and that inflated duration would read as an extremely slow, extremely hard set.

Timed holds are exempt. A plank logged as “1 rep, 60s” is a 60-second hold, not a forgotten watch, so a seconds-per-rep threshold must not be applied to it.

2 · What does the “weight” column actually mean?

Garmin records a number in weight for every set but never says what it represents. For a barbell it is the load. For a plank it is zero. For a weighted pull-up it is load added to bodyweight. Treating these alike is not cosmetic: it produced an all-NaN series for planks, because an estimated 1RM on a column that is always zero has no answer.

There is a cheap test for telling a real load from machine assistance. Under a real load, reps fall as weight rises; under assistance they rise, because more help means more reps. So the sign of the correlation should separate them.

Bespoke A pragmatic heuristic put together here, not a published method.
This test got pull-ups wrong, and the reason is worth keeping. Pull-ups came back at r = +0.39 while every other lift was negative, and were labelled “assisted” on that basis. They are in fact weighted pull-ups. The correlation was positive because Garmin's pull_up category lumps three different movements: bodyweight pull-ups at 0 lb, weighted pull-ups at ~25 lb added, and lat pulldowns at 47–120 lb of machine stack. Correlating across that mixture measures the difference between exercises, not a load–rep relationship within one. The test is only meaningful once the unit is a single movement — which is what the taxonomy below is for.

3 · The warmup problem

Most sets in a session are deliberately submaximal — warmups and back-off work. So the cloud of (reps, weight) points is dense well below true capacity. A regression through the middle of that cloud is not measuring what you can lift; it is measuring what you chose to lift, most of which was easy on purpose.

The fix is to fit a high quantile of the cloud instead of its centre. Drag the slider to watch the fitted line climb from the median toward the capacity envelope, and note how the implied rep-decay exponent \(\beta\) falls as it does.

Literature Quantile regression; the Bayesian form uses the Asymmetric Laplace device of Yu & Moyeed (2001).

4 · The load–rep curve

The relationship between load and achievable reps is modelled as a power law:

$$ w(r) \;=\; \text{1RM} \cdot r^{-\beta} $$

\(\beta\) controls how fast strength falls off as reps rise. It has a direct physical reading: a 10-rep max sits at \(10^{-\beta}\) of the one-rep max, so \(\beta = 0.15\) means a 10RM is about 71% of 1RM — which is what standard load-rep tables say.

Literature, extended This is Lombardi's (1989) power law. Lombardi fixes the exponent at 0.10; the only change here is estimating it from data. Set the slider to 0.10 below and our curve lands exactly on his.

Why the headline number is an 8-rep max, not a 1-rep max

A 1RM is the number everyone quotes, but it sits outside the data. Your sets are almost entirely 7–10 reps, so estimating a 1RM means extrapolating past the edge of what was ever observed, and the answer depends heavily on which formula is assumed. An e8RM interpolates, and its interval is correspondingly tighter — so that is what gets headlined, with e1RM shown alongside carrying the wider interval it honestly deserves.

5 · Combining variants onto one scale

“Is my bench progressing?” and “is my dumbbell bench progressing?” are different questions, and until this step only the second had an answer. Garmin records a dumbbell bench at a median 55 lb and a barbell bench at 135 lb. Plotted together they look like a strength collapse every time the implement changes; kept apart, neither series answers the question actually being asked.

Combining them needs a conversion factor, and there is no trustworthy one to borrow — published dumbbell:barbell ratios vary far too widely, and none of them are about this account. But the data can supply one, because over a long stretch both implements were trained in the same period, measuring the same underlying strength through two instruments.

The estimator

For movement family \(f\), on date \(t\), using variant \(v\)'s best set that day:

$$ \log(\text{e1RM}) \;=\; \text{level}_f(t) \;+\; \delta_v \;+\; \varepsilon $$

\(\text{level}_f(t)\) is the strength every variant shares, piecewise-linear between monthly knots; \(\delta_v\) is the systematic gap that remains after allowing for strength changing over time. Both fall out of one least-squares fit, and \(e^{-\delta_v}\) is the factor that puts variant \(v\) on the reference variant's scale. Fitting the level is what makes this different from a raw ratio: a trend running through the period would otherwise leak into the offset.

Bespoke The machinery is ordinary fixed-effects regression. Applying it to convert between lifting implements is a choice made here, and is not a published method.

The result

The fitted factors come out near 2–3× for dumbbell versus barbell work, which has a mundane explanation: Garmin logs dumbbell weight per hand, so two of them plus the biomechanical difference lands about there. That the estimate reproduces a known mechanical fact it was never told about is the first sign it is measuring something real.

Two checks, because one is not enough

Every factor is cross-checked against a completely independent estimate: pair each session of a variant with reference sessions within two weeks — close enough that real strength has barely moved — and take the median ratio. It uses far less data and says nothing about trend, but it fails in different ways, which is the point. Where the two agree, the factor is not an artefact of the level basis.

Agreement alone still is not sufficient. A label covering two different implements has no single valid factor, yet both estimators will happily converge on the midpoint of the mixture and agree with each other while both are wrong. That case is caught instead by residual spread, which is wide for a mixture and narrow for a genuine variant. A factor is used only if it passes both, plus a minimum of concurrent training history.

Two labels currently fail these checks and are excluded from the combined series rather than folded in on a number that is not believed — both are Garmin's unlabelled catch-all buckets, which genuinely do contain more than one implement. Their own per-variant series are unaffected.

6 · The model

For exercise \(e\), on date \(t\), for set \(i\):

$$ \log w_{eti} \;\sim\; \mathrm{AsymmetricLaplace}\!\left(\mu = \alpha_e(t) - \beta_e \log r_{eti},\; b,\; q\right) $$

The latent strength level \(\alpha_e(t)\)

Strength changes over weeks, not between consecutive sessions. So rather than giving every session its own free parameter, the level is a Gaussian random walk over a monthly knot grid, and each session reads a linear interpolation between the two knots surrounding it:

$$ \alpha_{e,k} \sim \mathcal{N}(\alpha_{e,k-1},\, \sigma_{\text{walk}}), \qquad \alpha_e(t) = (1-\lambda)\,\alpha_{e,k} + \lambda\,\alpha_{e,k+1} $$

This was not the original design — it replaced one node per session, which meant ~1,400 parameters. The knot grid cut that to 480 and imposes the smoothness directly. Use the slider to see what knot spacing does to the recovered level.

The rep-decay exponent \(\beta_e\)

\(\beta_e\) is partially pooled across exercises and squashed into a physiological band \([0.03, 0.35]\):

$$ \beta_e = \beta_{\min} + (\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min})\,\mathrm{logit}^{-1}\!\left(\mu_\beta + \sigma_\beta z_e\right) $$

The pooling is load-bearing rather than decorative. Where reps barely vary, \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are not separately identifiable at all — every set at 10 reps constrains only the combination \(\alpha - \beta\log 10\), so any increase in one buys an equal increase in the other. That is a ridge in the posterior rather than a peak. Exercises without enough rep spread therefore don't get their own \(\beta\) at all; they borrow the population value.

7 · Fitting & diagnostics

Sampled with NUTS (PyMC, numpyro backend). Convergence is judged by \(\hat{R}\), which compares variance between chains to variance within them; the conventional bar is \(\hat{R} \le 1.01\).

Literature Gelman & Rubin (1992); rank-normalised form and the 1.01 threshold from Vehtari et al. (2021).

What has been tried

Each bar is a real run. Lower is better; the dashed line is the 1.01 bar. Check the scope under each label — some are deliberate subsets, and comparing across scopes is what makes the pattern interpretable.

Validation on synthetic data

The model itself is verified against data generated from a known curve: it recovers a true \(\beta\) of 0.13 as 0.130, recovers the strength trajectory to within ~1%, converges cleanly (\(\hat{R} \approx 1.003\), zero divergences), and is not dragged down by adding warmup sets. So the open problem is the sampler's behaviour on this particular real dataset, not the model's logic.
